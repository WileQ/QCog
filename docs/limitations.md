# Limitations: what Q-Cog does not claim

A library that models human judgement carries an obligation to be explicit about the limits of what it models.
Every model in Q-Cog carries a `caveats` list that is printed by the CLI, shown in the explorer and embedded in
the report. This document collects the limits that apply to the library as a whole.

## On interpretation

* A quantum-probability model says that **the data are described by a quantum probability space**. It does not
  say that the brain computes with quantum amplitudes, that concepts are physically entangled, or that people
  are "using superposition". Nothing in this repository supports the stronger reading, and the models say so
  individually where the risk is highest (concept combination, conceptual entanglement, contradictory beliefs,
  memory, disjunction effect).
* Several quantum models fit *because* they have more freedom than a classical model with the same number of
  parameters. Where that matters, the library reports the classical baseline and information criteria rather
  than a p-value.
* A good fit is not evidence for a mechanism. The QQ equality is emphasised precisely because it has no free
  parameters; every other comparison in the library is a description, not a proof.

## On the models

* **Question order.** The QQ equality is defined for two dichotomous questions sharing the same two labels. For
  other question types the library says "not applicable" instead of quietly computing something meaningless.
* **Conjunction fallacy.** The effect requires the two attribute questions to be *incompatible*. The model runs
  the independent-attribute variant alongside and shows that the classical bound is then satisfied exactly, so a
  reader can see that the fallacy is not about uncertainty as such.
* **Disjunction effect.** The model proves that no bipartite "world x action" state - however entangled - can
  reproduce the effect, so the effect is a claim about how the uncertainty is *represented*. That is a
  mathematical result about the model, not an empirical finding about people.
* **Concept combination / conceptual entanglement.** Concurrence and entanglement entropy are properties of the
  modelled state. They quantify joint structure; they do not diagnose anything about a person.
* **Game theory.** Conventions for the entangling gate and strategy phases vary between papers, and the "(Q,Q) is
  a Nash equilibrium" claim depends on the strategy space assumed. Q-Cog documents its convention, verifies the
  payoff-dominance and classical-limit properties, and prints the best-response scan instead of asserting an
  equilibrium it cannot check. `evolution-of-cooperation` shows that which strategy survives depends on the
  convention: in this library's convention the surviving cooperative strategy is the classical one, not `Q`.
* **Population models.** The dense simulation supports at most ten agents. Larger networks need the classical
  baselines included in `qcog.core.classical`, or a tensor-network implementation. The trajectories are
  deterministic given the initial state (no measurement randomness between rounds), which is a modelling choice,
  not a limitation of the formalism.
* **Collective judgement.** Correlation is modelled as a GHZ-style state mixed with a product state. Real groups
  correlate for many reasons and the model does not distinguish between them.
* **Information cascades.** The social context is modelled as a rotation of the belief rather than as Bayesian
  evidence. The model is about order-sensitive decision making, not about rationality.
* **Voting.** Pairwise (Condorcet) comparisons are coherent comparisons of two options; with this construction
  the majority relation comes out transitive, and the model reports whether a cycle occurred rather than
  assuming one. A cycle requires context-dependent pairwise questions or aggregation across voters.
* **Categorisation.** The Zeno-like stabilisation is a property of repeated measurement; it does not require the
  labels to carry new information.

## On the data

* The datasets in `src/qcog/data/` are **illustrative synthetic data generated by the library itself**, with a
  fixed seed. They are not the published values from the cited papers. Their role is to exercise the fitting
  workflow; see `src/qcog/data/README.md`.
* Real data should be attributed to its source in the project page, not just in a file.
* No human-subjects data is included, and none should be contributed without documented ethics review and
  consent (see `CODE_OF_CONDUCT.md`).

## On the implementation

* The analytic engine is exact for the models in this library (dimensions up to a handful of qubits). Larger
  systems need sparse or tensor-network methods; the library does not pretend to scale.
* Circuit compilation costs grow with the product of the answer options, because the prefix construction
  enumerates answer prefixes. This is fine for the two- and three-question designs in the catalogue and is stated
  where it matters.
* Fitting uses a local optimiser (Levenberg-Marquardt style, bounded). Multi-modal landscapes are possible; the
  fitter reports its success flag, cost and residuals, and the documentation tells you to check them. A fit that
  hit a bound is not reported as an estimate of a mechanism.
* Figures are static images. The accessible equivalent is the data table that accompanies every chart.
