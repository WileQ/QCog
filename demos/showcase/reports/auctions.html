Bid after a high valuation 0.848 versus 0.076 after a low one; order shift -0.034.

What this model asks

Why are bids correlated with the order in which a bidder thinks about the item's value and about the competition?

How it works

A three-level valuation question and a dichotomous bidding question that do not commute; a unitary rotation represents the information about rivals.

Classical baseline. Risk-neutral bidding: the bid is a deterministic function of the (estimated) value, so no presentation order can change it.

References

  • Busemeyer & Bruza (2012), Quantum Models of Cognition and Decision, ch. 12 (economic applications).
  • Thaler (1988), Anomalies: the winner's curse, Journal of Economic Perspectives 2, 191-202.

What this run shows

  • After answering that the item is high-value, the bidder bids high with probability 0.848; after a low valuation, with probability 0.076. The bidding question is genuinely sensitive to the valuation answer.
  • Reversing the order changes the valuation marginal by -0.034, which the order-invariant classical baseline cannot produce.
  • Thinking about rival bidders shifts the high bid by +0.173 in this model: rival information acts as a rotation of the belief, not as a change of the value estimate.
  • The practical warning is the classical one - a bidder whose bid depends on the order of their own deliberation cannot know whether the bid or the valuation was primary.

Parameters: valuation_confidence = 0.55, rival_pressure = 0.6, bid_aggressiveness = 0.8. Backend: exact (exact). Runtime 0.0046 s.

What this does not claim

  • This is a model of response probabilities, not a claim about what the brain does.
  • A quantum model only means the data are described by a quantum probability space; classical models may fit equally well, and the classical baseline is reported for that reason.
  • The valuation question is a three-outcome model of a continuous estimate; the third outcome stands for the high-value region.
