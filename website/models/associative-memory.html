Associative memory and competing retrieval cues

module B: Logic, concepts and memoryintermediateslug: associative-memory

Cue-based recall 0.822 versus 0.820 unconditionally; cue similarity drives the interference.

What it asks

Why does adding a second, similar retrieval cue sometimes reduce rather than increase the chance of recall?

How it works

Cues as measurements with different bases: two similar cues are close in Hilbert space, so the paths they open to the same trace interfere.

Classical baseline. Independent-cue models: the probability of recall under both cues follows from the single-cue probabilities by the law of total probability.

What this run shows

  • Recall probability with the cue answered 'yes' is 0.822, against 0.820 unconditionally: the cue moves recall.
  • Because the recall question is not compatible with the cue, the two retrieval paths interfere; the size of that interference is the number the library reports rather than the sum of the separate effects.
  • Asking the other question first moves p(cue = yes) by -0.316 (TV distance 0.3156). Question incompatibility = 2.1633.
  • QQ equality holds: same-answer agreement is 0.8221 in one order and 0.8221 in the other (difference +0.0000, z = +0.00).
  • Law of total probability violated: p(cue=yes) measured directly = 0.9975, but computed from the other order = 0.6819 (violation +0.3156)
  • Law of total probability violated: p(cue=no) measured directly = 0.0025, but computed from the other order = 0.3181 (violation -0.3156)
  • The classical (law-of-total-probability) part of p(recall=yes) is 0.820 and the interference term is -0.038, i.e. interference supplies -4.9% of the probability. Interference can be negative, which is what makes the two question orders disagree.
  • A classical model answers both questions from a single joint distribution, so it predicts a marginal shift of exactly 0; the model predicts 0.3156. The (unfitted) classical baseline is therefore rejected by construction whenever this is nonzero - which is a statement about the model, not yet about people.

What this does not claim

  • This is a model of response probabilities, not a claim about what the brain does.
  • A quantum model only means the data are described by a quantum probability space; classical models may fit equally well, and the classical baseline is reported for that reason.
  • The model describes recall probabilities, not the neural mechanism of retrieval.
