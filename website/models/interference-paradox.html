Interference between unresolved alternatives (disjunction effect)

module A: Individual cognition and decision makingintermediateslug: interference-paradox

Interference supplies 50.0% of p(decision = yes); distinguishing the alternatives shifts it by +0.500.

What it asks

Why do people who would accept a gamble conditional on winning and conditional on losing decline it while the outcome is still unknown (Tversky & Shafir's disjunction effect)?

How it works

Interference between the amplitudes of unresolved alternatives; a recorded intermediate question acts as a non-selective measurement that destroys the off-diagonal terms.

Classical baseline. The law of total probability / sure-thing principle requires p(decide yes) = p(win) p(yes|win) + p(lose) p(yes|lose) exactly.

What this run shows

  • With the alternatives combined, p(decision = yes) = 1.000; with them fully distinguished it is 0.500 (change +0.500), and with the requested partial which-path visibility it is 1.000.
  • The classical (law-of-total-probability) part of p(decision=yes) is 0.500 and the interference term is +0.500, i.e. interference supplies 50.0% of the probability. Interference can be negative, which is what makes the two question orders disagree.
  • Sure-thing check: the direct probability differs from the branch-weighted average by +0.500, where the classical model requires exactly zero.
  • The visibility parameter moves continuously between the two cases, which is why this is best read as a which-path experiment rather than as two parallel minds.

What this does not claim

  • This is a model of response probabilities, not a claim about what the brain does.
  • A quantum model only means the data are described by a quantum probability space; classical models may fit equally well, and the classical baseline is reported for that reason.
  • A violation of the sure-thing principle shows that respondents did not integrate both branches into one probability space; it does not by itself identify interference.
