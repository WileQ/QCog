Question-order effects and incompatible questions

module A: Individual cognition and decision makingintermediateslug: question-order

Order effect 0.289; QQ difference +0.000; incompatibility 1.634.

What it asks

Why does the answer to 'how satisfied are you with your life?' depend on whether 'how satisfied are you with your marriage?' was asked first?

How it works

Sequential projective (or unsharp) measurements of non-commuting questions: answering the first question projects the state, so the second question is put to a different belief state.

Classical baseline. A single joint distribution over responses (a classical sample space) forces the marginal of a question to be identical in both orders; any nonzero order effect rejects that family of models.

What this run shows

  • Asking the other question first moves p(A = yes) by -0.289 (TV distance 0.2888). Question incompatibility = 1.6337.
  • QQ equality holds: same-answer agreement is 0.9082 in one order and 0.9082 in the other (difference +0.0000, z = +0.00).
  • Law of total probability violated: p(A=yes) measured directly = 0.7888, but computed from the other order = 0.5000 (violation +0.2888)
  • Law of total probability violated: p(A=no) measured directly = 0.2112, but computed from the other order = 0.5000 (violation -0.2888)
  • The classical (law-of-total-probability) part of p(B=no) is 0.264 and the interference term is +0.236, i.e. interference supplies 47.2% of the probability. Interference can be negative, which is what makes the two question orders disagree.
  • A classical model answers both questions from a single joint distribution, so it predicts a marginal shift of exactly 0; the model predicts 0.2888. The (unfitted) classical baseline is therefore rejected by construction whenever this is nonzero - which is a statement about the model, not yet about people.
  • The sharpness sweep makes the classical limit explicit: as the questions become uninformative, both the order effect and the interference term go to zero, so the quantum structure comes from the questions being incompatible, not from the state alone.

What this does not claim

  • This is a model of response probabilities, not a claim about what the brain does.
  • A quantum model only means the data are described by a quantum probability space; classical models may fit equally well, and the classical baseline is reported for that reason.
  • The QQ equality requires two dichotomous questions; for more outcomes the model checks the corresponding multi-term identity numerically instead.
