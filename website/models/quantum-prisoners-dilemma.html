Quantum prisoner's dilemma (EWL scheme)

module C: Strategic decisions and game theoryintermediateslug: quantum-prisoners-dilemma

Q vs Q: payoffs (3.00, 3.00), cooperation 1.00, entanglement 1.57 rad, quantum advantage +2.000.

What it asks

Can a shared, correlated context make cooperation rational between partners who are each tempted to defect?

How it works

The Eisert-Wilkert-Lewenstein scheme: an entangling gate before the strategy unitaries and its inverse after, so the players can use non-classical strategies that the entanglement makes coherent.

Classical baseline. The classical prisoner's dilemma: defection strictly dominates, mutual defection is the unique Nash equilibrium, and both players receive the punishment payoff.

What this run shows

  • Chosen profile Q vs Q: quantum payoffs (3.00, 3.00) against classical Nash benchmark (D, D), because Q has no classical counterpart (1.00, 1.00).
  • With maximal entanglement the classical temptation disappears: a player who defects while the other cooperates receives 0.000 instead of the classical 5, so the entangling context removes the reason to defect against a cooperator.
  • Against a Q-playing partner the best-response scan over the full two-parameter strategy family finds a payoff of 5.000 (at theta = 3.14, phi = 0.00), while Q against Q yields 3.000. The library reports the scan rather than asserting that Q is a Nash equilibrium, because that claim depends on which strategy space the players are allowed to use - still an open point in the quantum-games literature, and the model's caveats say so.
  • Classical analysis: Any mixture with positive defection probability weakly dominates cooperation, so mutual defection is the unique Nash equilibrium.
  • Sweeping the entanglement shows the cooperation rate rising from 0 (classical game) to 1 (maximal entanglement); decoherence undoes it, returning the model to the classical prediction.

What this does not claim

  • This is a model of response probabilities, not a claim about what the brain does.
  • A quantum model only means the data are described by a quantum probability space; classical models may fit equally well, and the classical baseline is reported for that reason.
  • This is a game-theoretic model: 'players' need not be people, and the entanglement is part of the game's rules rather than a claim about negotiation between humans.
  • Sign and phase conventions for the entangling gate differ between papers, and the claim that (Q,Q) is a Nash equilibrium is convention-dependent. This library documents its own convention, verifies the statements it makes (payoff dominance, the disappearance of the temptation to defect, and the exact classical limit at zero entanglement) and prints the best-response scan instead of asserting an equilibrium it cannot check.
