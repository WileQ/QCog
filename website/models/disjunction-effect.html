Disjunction effect and the representation of unresolved alternatives

module B: Logic, concepts and memoryadvancedslug: disjunction-effect

Unknown opponent: 0.988 versus the sure-thing prediction 0.700 (+0.288); the bipartite representation gives 0.700.

What it asks

Why is the decision different only when the alternatives are unresolved, if the decision is the same in each resolved branch?

How it works

Coherent amplitudes for the two unresolved branches adding to the same decision state, versus the incoherent branch mixture produced by any tensor-product representation.

Classical baseline. The sure-thing principle: p(act) must equal the branch-weighted average of the conditional act probabilities. It is also the exact prediction of any bipartite (world x action) state.

What this run shows

  • Cooperation when the opponent is unknown: 0.988 (coherent representation) versus 0.700 (branch mixture = sure-thing prediction). Difference +0.288.
  • The bipartite representation reproduces the sure-thing value to within 2.22e-16 both when the opponent's move is unknown and when it is revealed: no amount of entanglement between world and action can produce the effect.
  • The relative-phase sweep moves the effect from constructive (more cooperation under uncertainty) through zero to destructive, which is the observable signature of coherence between the branches.
  • Reading: the disjunction effect is not a failure of rationality to be patched by extra states - it is a constraint on how the uncertainty is represented.

What this does not claim

  • This is a model of response probabilities, not a claim about what the brain does.
  • A quantum model only means the data are described by a quantum probability space; classical models may fit equally well, and the classical baseline is reported for that reason.
  • The model demonstrates that the effect requires a non-reducible representation; it does not show that people use one.
