Quantum decision tree: does the order of information matter?

module A: Individual cognition and decision makingadvancedslug: decision-tree

Information order changes the final decision by 0.126.

What it asks

Two people receive the same three pieces of evidence in different orders. Do they reach the same decision, and if not, which piece of evidence should have come first?

How it works

A sequence of measurements: each cue projects the belief state, so later cues act on a state that the earlier cues have already changed.

Classical baseline. Classical belief updating (Bayes' rule) is commutative: the posterior does not depend on the order in which the evidence arrived.

What this run shows

  • Order cue1 -> cue2 -> cue3 ends with p(positive) = 0.478; the reversed order ends with 0.604. Divergence = 0.126.
  • A Bayesian decision tree predicts a divergence of exactly zero for the same cues, so the whole effect comes from the non-commutativity of the cues.
  • The divergence is largest when the most incompatible cue is asked of the state it least agrees with, which the per-cue tables show directly.
  • Practical reading: when a decision is order-sensitive, the order that asks the most incompatible question last is the one that preserves the earlier judgements.

What this does not claim

  • This is a model of response probabilities, not a claim about what the brain does.
  • A quantum model only means the data are described by a quantum probability space; classical models may fit equally well, and the classical baseline is reported for that reason.
  • Three cues are simulated; this demonstrates order dependence rather than calibrating a specific task.
